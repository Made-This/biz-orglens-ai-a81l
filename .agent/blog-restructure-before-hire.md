## The $300k hire that lasted six months

A founder I know hired a VP of Sales last spring. Strong résumé. Two prior wins at companies in the same revenue band. References that genuinely lit up on the phone.

She started on a Monday. By Tuesday, she discovered that "sales" at the company was actually four people: the founder (who still owned the top five accounts), a growth marketer running outbound, a senior AE who'd been promised the team, and a customer success lead doing renewals that were sometimes new sales. Nobody knew who owned what. The VP spent her first 90 days trying to redraw the lines. The founder kept overriding her on big deals. The senior AE quit. Six months in, the VP left too — citing "lack of role clarity."

The founder did the math afterward. Recruiting fees, salary, equity vesting, lost momentum, replacement cost: north of $300k. And the underlying problem — unclear ownership between founder, growth, and sales — was still there.

This is the most common, most expensive mistake a 20-to-80-person company makes. And it's almost always avoidable with a brief organizational review *before* the requisition gets posted.

## The hidden cost of hiring into confusion

Adding a senior leader to an unclear org doesn't fix the org. It amplifies whatever was broken.

If ownership was fuzzy, a new senior hire walks into a political minefield on day one. If the founder was the bottleneck, the new hire becomes a more expensive bottleneck-translator. If the role overlaps with two existing functions, the new person spends their first quarter negotiating turf instead of executing.

The pattern shows up at predictable inflection points. Around 25 people. Again at 50. Again at 80. Each time, execution slows, and the founder's instinct is the same: *we need more leverage, let's hire a leader.* Sometimes that's right. Often it isn't.

The question isn't whether you need help. You almost certainly do. The question is whether a new hire will actually absorb the work, or whether they'll bounce off a structure that wasn't ready for them.

## 5 signals you need a structural review, not a hire

If two or more of these feel familiar, pause the job post for a week. Look at the org first.

- **You're still the bottleneck after hiring two or more managers.** Decisions still route through you. Approvals stack up when you're traveling. Your calendar is the rate-limiter on the company. Adding a third manager won't change this — the structure will.
- **Roles keep expanding to cover gaps, not by design.** Your head of ops now also owns vendor management, light HR, and half of finance. Your senior engineer is doing recruiting. People are stretching because nobody else owns the work, not because the role was designed that way.
- **A previous key hire left or underperformed despite strong credentials.** They had the résumé. References checked out. They still didn't work. That's a signal about the seat, not the person.
- **You can't clearly articulate what the new hire would own that isn't already owned.** Try writing the one-line scope on a sticky note. If it overlaps with two existing leaders, or if you find yourself writing "and helps with...", the role isn't ready to be filled.
- **The team is growing but velocity is slowing.** More headcount, less output per person, longer cycle times. This is the clearest signal that the constraint is structural, not capacity.

Two or more of these, and the problem is almost certainly not headcount. It's structure.

## What a structural review actually looks like

This part doesn't have to be heavy. You're not running a McKinsey engagement. You're answering three or four questions honestly.

### 1. Who owns each outcome — not each task, each outcome?

Pick the five most important business outcomes for the next two quarters. Revenue. Retention. A specific product milestone. Hiring. Whatever they are for you. For each one, write down a single name. If you can't, or if two names compete, you've found a leadership gap.

### 2. Where is the founder still the single point of failure?

List every decision in the last 30 days that genuinely required you. Be honest. Now sort them: which truly need the founder, and which exist because nobody else has the authority or context? The second list is your bottleneck inventory.

### 3. Are your current leaders in the right seats?

For each manager you already have, ask: if I were hiring for this seat today, knowing what I know now, would I hire this person? It's a brutal question. It also reveals more than any review cycle. A wrong-seat situation doesn't get fixed by adding a peer above or below — it gets fixed by moving the person.

### 4. What's the smallest structural change that would unblock the next two quarters?

Sometimes the answer is "hire a VP of Sales." Often it's smaller: clarify that the senior AE owns mid-market end-to-end, take the top accounts off the founder's plate, redraw the line between growth and sales. Cheaper, faster, and frequently sufficient.

## When hiring is the right answer

To be clear: sometimes you really do need to hire. If you've answered the four questions above, ownership is clean, no one is in the wrong seat, the founder isn't the bottleneck, and you can write the new role's scope on a sticky note without flinching — go hire. Quickly. Aggressively.

The point of this checklist isn't to delay hires. It's to make sure the next senior hire lands in a structure that lets them succeed. The cost of hiring into clarity is a great new leader. The cost of hiring into confusion is six months, three hundred grand, and the same problem still sitting on your desk.

## A founder-to-founder closing thought

Hiring vs. restructuring isn't either/or. It's a sequence. Restructure first when the signals say structure. Hire first when the signals say capacity. Most founders default to capacity because hiring feels like action. Restructuring feels like delay. It isn't — it's the highest-leverage move you'll make this quarter.
